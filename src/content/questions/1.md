---
title: The Lack of Beautiful Spaces
description: A summer between Greenwich Village and Chelsea.
---

This summer I lived in New York, right by Union Square, a short walk from both Greenwich Village and Chelsea. Some of these buildings are from the 1850s, and they still feel better than most things built since.

![Commerce Street in Greenwich Village](/images/questions/greenwich-commerce-street.jpg)

*Commerce Street, Greenwich Village. Photo by [Joe Mabel](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Manhattan_-_south_side_of_Commerce_Street_east_of_Bedford_Street.jpg), [CC BY-SA 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/).*

Where I am now, most of what gets built feels like it was designed to be fine. Parking lots, beige apartment blocks, strip malls. Nothing about it is wrong exactly, but nobody would go on a walk just to look at it.

![A brownstone with an iron gate on West 9th Street](/images/questions/greenwich-west-9th.jpg)

*A brownstone from 1854 on West 9th Street, Greenwich Village. Photo by [Spencer Means](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Brownstone_(1854)_with_front_garden_and_iron_gate,_West_9th_Street,_Greenwich_Village,_New_York_City_(22614777044).jpg), [CC BY-SA 2.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/2.0/).*

What confuses me is that we're far richer than the people who built these streets, with better tools and materials. Yet somewhere along the way, caring about how a place looks and feels dropped out of the process. Is it cost? Zoning and building codes? Designing for cars instead of people on foot? Or did we just stop believing beauty was worth paying for?

![Rowhouses on West 22nd Street in Chelsea](/images/questions/chelsea-west-22nd.jpg)

*Rowhouses on West 22nd Street, Chelsea. Photo by [Beyond My Ken](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:489_-_469_West_22nd_Street.jpg), [CC BY-SA 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/).*

I don't know the answer yet. But living there made me notice how much a place can change how you feel just walking through it.
